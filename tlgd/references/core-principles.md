# Core principles

## Apply the guide as house style

- Treat the guide as a coherent house style, not an objective industry standard or a replacement for project-specific guidance.
- Use this hierarchy: explicit user and project guidance, this guide, then the preferred outside references.
- Break a guideline when doing so materially improves clarity for the audience. Apply the exception consistently.
- Ask what the reader is trying to accomplish. Optimize for successful task completion, not for sounding formal or impressive.

## Write prescriptively and accurately

- Recommend the clearest path for the common use case instead of presenting every possible option.
- State a specific purpose and align headings, scenarios, examples, procedures, and commands with it.
- Distinguish requirements, recommendations, options, outcomes, and possibilities:
  - Use **must** or an imperative for required actions.
  - Use **we recommend** or name the recommending organization for recommendations.
  - Use **can** for ability or an optional action.
  - Use **might** for a possible outcome.
  - Avoid ambiguous **should** unless the recommendation is broadly recognized.
- Do not make unverifiable or fragile claims about performance, cost, security, competitors, or quality. Avoid **best**, **fastest**, **simplest**, **always**, **never**, **ensure**, and **guarantee** unless evidence supports the exact claim.
- Cite the source for measurable claims. Describe security features as helping, reducing risk, or being designed for a purpose rather than guaranteeing an outcome.
- Do not document or imply unannounced future features without the required legal approval.
- Write timeless product documentation. Remove **currently**, **now**, **new**, **latest**, **soon**, **existing**, and similar time anchors unless the content is inherently time-stamped, such as release notes.
- Paraphrase and link to third-party content instead of copying text, images, code, logos, or speech unless reuse rights are confirmed.

## Use the Google voice

- Sound like a knowledgeable, respectful colleague: conversational, direct, natural, and useful.
- Be friendly without slang, cuteness, hype, excessive entertainment, or unnecessary formality.
- Keep the reader's information need primary. Do not bury the answer under throat-clearing or a style lecture.
- Use common contractions, especially negative contractions, when they improve naturalness and scanability.
- Do not add **please** to routine instructions. Avoid **please note**, **at this time**, **let's**, internet slang, pop-culture references, and exclamation marks.
- Do not label tasks **easy**, **simple**, **obvious**, or **quick**. Give the steps, prerequisites, or realistic duration instead.

## Design for a global and inclusive audience

- Default to US English, while assuming that many readers use English as an additional language or will read a translation.
- Prefer literal, common, precise words. Avoid idioms, metaphors, wordplay, culture-specific references, seasonal assumptions, and unnecessary phrasal verbs.
- Use the same term for the same concept. Do not introduce synonyms merely for variety.
- Avoid unnecessarily gendered, ableist, violent, dehumanizing, stigmatizing, or exclusionary language.
- Respect how communities describe themselves. Do not contrast people with disabilities against people described as **normal** or **healthy**.
- Use diverse, non-stereotyped examples and singular **they** for a person whose gender is unknown or irrelevant.
- Never expose real personal, confidential, credential, or customer information in examples or screenshots.

## Make the content accessible

- Make every instruction and meaning available without depending only on color, location, shape, size, sound, images, motion, or punctuation.
- Use semantic structure, descriptive headings and links, short focused paragraphs, parallel lists, real table headers, text alternatives, and keyboard-accessible controls.
- Avoid directional references such as **above**, **below**, **left**, and **right**. Name the page, section, control, diagram, or use **preceding** and **following**.
- Keep sentences under about 26 words when practical, but do not create choppy prose to meet a number.
- Put distinguishing or critical information first so readers can scan with a screen reader or visually.
- Ensure errors state what happened and how to fix it.

## Review in priority order

Check technical accuracy, completeness, reader goal, organization, actionability, clarity, accessibility, inclusivity, terminology, grammar, punctuation, and formatting—in that order. A polished sentence that gives the wrong instruction is still wrong.
